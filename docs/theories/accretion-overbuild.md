# Theory E: Accretion Overbuild — Operational Deep Dive

## What You Are Actually Building

Forget the finished pyramid for a moment. Under the accretion overbuild theory, the first thing you build at Giza is not a pyramid at all. It is a **massive stepped trapezoid** — a blocky, terraced mountain of limestone with integrated ramps running up its faces. It looks nothing like a pyramid. It looks like a ziggurat that ate another ziggurat.

The final pyramid shape exists only as survey lines carved into the core blocks at intervals — invisible reference planes buried inside the mass. The elegant geometry everyone associates with Giza does not appear until the **carve-down phase**, when skilled masons work from the apex downward, peeling away the overbuild to expose the true faces.

The structure you build has three components:

1. **The core pyramid volume** — the ~2.6 million cubic meters of stone that will remain as the finished Great Pyramid.
2. **The overbuild mass** — the excess stone surrounding and above the core, shaped to provide working surfaces and integrated ramps. This is not scaffolding. It is structural, load-bearing stonework indistinguishable from the core.
3. **The integrated ramps** — ramps that climb the terraced faces at usable gradients (~7-8%), built from the same material as the overbuild. They are not additions to the structure; they ARE the structure. When the overbuild is carved away, the ramps go with it.

### Physical Geometry

Imagine the Great Pyramid's base (230.3m per side) expanded outward by 15-25 meters on each face, with the overbuild rising in stepped courses. Each step is ~1-2 meters high. The integrated ramps zigzag up the stepped faces, gaining ~1 meter of height for every 12-14 meters of ramp length (a ~7% grade, comfortable for loaded sledge teams).

The top of the trapezoid during the build-up phase is a broad, flat working platform — perhaps 30-40 meters across at the level where the pyramid's apex will eventually be. Blocks are delivered to this platform and worked downward to fill each course. The structure grows upward layer by layer, with each new terrace slightly narrower than the one below, but always wide enough to support ramp access and working space.

## The Overbuild Mass: How Much Extra Stone?

This is where the arithmetic gets interesting.

### Great Pyramid Numbers

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Finished volume | ~2.6 million m³ |
| Finished mass | ~6.1 million tons |
| Overbuild volume (estimated) | ~1.0-1.3 million m³ |
| Overbuild mass (estimated) | ~2.3-3.0 million tons |
| Total trapezoid mass at peak | ~8.4-9.1 million tons |
| Overbuild as % of finished pyramid | ~38-50% |

That overbuild figure — roughly 2.5 million tons — is not a random number. It aligns remarkably well with the mass requirements of the next structure in the Giza sequence.

### Khafre's Pyramid

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Base | 215.3m per side |
| Height | 143.5m |
| Estimated mass | ~5.2 million tons |
| Khafre's overbuild mass (estimated) | ~1.5-2.0 million tons |

The Great Pyramid's overbuild mass (~2.5M tons) closely matches what you need to build Khafre's pyramid core (~5.2M tons) when you account for:
- Khafre has its own local quarry contributing bulk core stone.
- The overbuild material from the Great Pyramid is already cut, dressed, and standardized — it goes to Khafre's build-up as premium pre-processed stone.
- Khafre's own overbuild then cascades downward to Menkaure.

### Menkaure's Pyramid

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Base | 102.1m per side |
| Height | 65.5m |
| Estimated mass | ~0.59 million tons |

Menkaure is dramatically smaller — about 1/10 the volume of the Great Pyramid. Khafre's overbuild (~1.5-2.0M tons) vastly exceeds Menkaure's core needs. The surplus feeds the mortuary temples, valley temples, causeways, enclosure walls, queens' pyramids, and the extensive mastaba fields of the Giza complex. These non-pyramid structures account for an estimated 1.0-1.5 million tons of stone.

### The Mass Accounting

```
Quarried for Great Pyramid trapezoid:  ~8.5M tons
  → Remains as Great Pyramid:          6.1M tons
  → Carved off as overbuild:           2.4M tons
    → Feeds Khafre build-up:           (partial, supplemented by local quarry)

Khafre trapezoid total:                ~6.7-7.2M tons
  → Remains as Khafre:                 5.2M tons
  → Carved off:                        1.5-2.0M tons
    → Feeds Menkaure + complex:

Menkaure trapezoid total:              ~0.8-1.0M tons
  → Remains as Menkaure:               0.59M tons
  → Carved off:                        0.2-0.4M tons
    → Feeds temples, causeways, walls

Total quarried from bedrock:           ≈ Total complex mass
Waste:                                 Minimal
```

The punchline: **total quarried material equals total complex mass**. No mysterious surplus quarry. No missing stone. No ramp material that vanished. Every block quarried from the Giza plateau or shipped from Tura is accounted for in the finished complex.

## Integrated Ramps: The Ramp IS the Building

In every other ramp theory, the ramp is a parasite on the project. An external straight ramp to reach 146 meters at 7% gradient would be 1.6 kilometers long and contain roughly 2.6 million cubic meters of material — nearly the volume of the pyramid itself. You build two structures, then demolish one.

The accretion overbuild theory dissolves this problem by making the ramp **synonymous with the overbuild**. The stepped terraces of the trapezoid provide the ramp surfaces. No separate structure is built and no separate structure needs to be removed.

### How the Ramps Work

Picture the trapezoid during construction. Each face is a staircase of stone courses, each step 1-2 meters high and 3-5 meters deep. The integrated ramps run diagonally up these steps, built from the same limestone blocks as the rest of the structure:

- **Lower courses (0-50m height)**: Ramps are broad — 8-10 meters wide — accommodating multiple sledge teams working in parallel. The gradient is gentle (~5-6%) because there is ample horizontal space. This is where throughput matters most, since the lower 50 meters contain roughly 70% of the pyramid's total volume.
- **Middle courses (50-100m)**: Ramps narrow to 4-6 meters. Gradient steepens slightly (~7-8%). Fewer parallel sledge tracks, but the volume of stone being placed per course drops dramatically as the structure narrows.
- **Upper courses (100-146m)**: Ramps are 2-3 meters wide, single-sledge-track. Gradient can approach 10%. By this height, each course contains only a few hundred blocks. The narrow ramps suffice because throughput requirements have dropped proportionally.

The ramps zigzag up the faces, switching direction at each terrace. Where they turn, the landing platforms are built wider — stone "plazas" at each switchback providing space for sledge teams to maneuver.

The critical insight: **the ramp gradient problem scales with height, but so does the volume reduction**. You need the widest, most gradual ramps exactly where there is the most overbuild mass available to build them from. As the structure narrows and ramp space shrinks, you need to move far less stone.

## Construction Sequence

### Phase 1: Foundation and Lower Courses (Years 1-5)

The Giza plateau itself is prepared: leveled, with a perimeter trench cut to establish the base plane. The local limestone quarry immediately south of the pyramid site opens. Tura quarries across the Nile begin producing fine casing stone.

The first courses are laid in expanding rectangles, each course slightly wider than the final pyramid base to accommodate the overbuild. Foundation courses extend perhaps 15-20 meters beyond the eventual pyramid faces on each side.

At this stage, the structure looks like a low, broad, stepped platform. Integrated ramps begin as gentle slopes on all four faces. Blocks are hauled up simple inclines less than 5 meters high. The bottleneck is quarrying speed, not vertical transport.

**Volume placed in this phase**: roughly 3.5-4.0 million tons (including overbuild), representing 40-45% of the total trapezoid mass. The bottom third of the pyramid by height contains over two-thirds of its volume.

### Phase 2: Middle Courses (Years 5-12)

The trapezoid is now a substantial terraced mountain, perhaps 60-80 meters tall. The integrated ramps are well-established zigzag paths up the faces. Sledge teams haul blocks up ramp systems with total path lengths of 400-800 meters to reach the working courses.

The workforce is at peak size — 25,000-30,000 during flood season, 8,000-10,000 year-round. Multiple ramp lines on each face allow parallel hauling. The quarry, transport, and placement operations are mature and running at steady-state capacity.

Internal chambers (the King's Chamber, Queen's Chamber, Grand Gallery) are built during this phase, since they sit within the lower-middle portion of the pyramid's core. The 25-80 ton granite beams for the relieving chambers above the King's Chamber are the most challenging lifts — hauled up the integrated ramps on reinforced sledge tracks, possibly with the assistance of counterweight systems in the Grand Gallery.

**Volume placed in this phase**: roughly 3.0-3.5 million tons (including overbuild).

### Phase 3: Upper Courses and Capstone (Years 12-16)

The trapezoid approaches its maximum height. The working platform at the top narrows. Ramps become single-track. Daily block count drops from hundreds to dozens, but each block requires more effort to place.

The capstone (pyramidion) — possibly gilded or electrum-sheathed — is placed at the apex. The trapezoid has reached its maximum extent: a massive, stepped, flat-topped mountain roughly 150+ meters tall.

**Volume placed in this phase**: roughly 1.5-2.0 million tons (including overbuild).

### Phase 4: Carve-Down (Years 14-20)

This is the phase that makes Theory E unique. Starting from the top, skilled masons begin removing the overbuild to reveal the final pyramid faces.

The carve-down begins while the upper courses of the build-up are still being completed — the two phases overlap. Masons can begin carving the upper overbuild as soon as the apex is established and downward survey lines are set.

The carve-down proceeds top-down for critical geometric reasons:
- Survey lines are established from the apex (the single known point) downward.
- Working from the top, masons can use plumb lines and sighting rods to maintain the precise 51°50'40" face angle.
- Gravity assists material removal — blocks are lowered or rolled down, not hauled up.
- Each newly exposed face course provides the reference line for the next course below it.

**Volume removed during carve-down**: ~2.3-3.0 million tons.

**Where it goes**: Down the still-intact ramps on the unfinished faces, onto sledges, to staging areas, then transported to Khafre's construction site (or stockpiled if Khafre's build-up has not yet begun absorbing material at full rate).

## Cascading Material: The DAG

This is the architectural insight that transforms Giza from three separate projects into a single interconnected system.

### Hub-and-Spoke vs. Supply Web

Under any other theory, each pyramid is a **hub-and-spoke** project:

```
Quarry A ──→ Great Pyramid
Quarry B ──→ Great Pyramid
Quarry C ──→ Great Pyramid

Quarry A ──→ Khafre (later, separately)
Quarry B ──→ Khafre
...
```

Every block comes from a quarry. Every pyramid draws independently from the same sources. The quarries are the bottleneck, and the projects compete for quarry output.

Under Theory E, the material flow is a **directed acyclic graph**:

```
Quarries ──→ Great Pyramid Trapezoid
                    │
                    │ (carve-down output)
                    ▼
Quarries ──→ Khafre Trapezoid
                    │
                    │ (carve-down output)
                    ▼
Quarries ──→ Menkaure Trapezoid
                    │
                    │ (carve-down output)
                    ▼
              Temples, Causeways,
              Walls, Mastabas
```

Each structure has two input streams: **fresh quarry stone** and **cascading overbuild material** from the upstream structure. The quarry burden on each downstream project is reduced by the overbuild output flowing in.

### The Timing Cascade

For this DAG to work, the phases must overlap:

| Year | Great Pyramid | Khafre | Menkaure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-5 | Build-up (lower) | — | — |
| 5-12 | Build-up (middle/upper) | Site prep | — |
| 12-16 | Build-up (top) + Carve begins | Build-up (lower) | — |
| 16-20 | Carve-down (full) | Build-up (middle) | — |
| 20-24 | Carve-down (finishing) | Build-up (upper) + Carve begins | Site prep |
| 24-30 | Complete | Carve-down | Build-up |
| 30-35 | — | Carve-down (finishing) | Build-up + Carve begins |
| 35-40 | — | Complete | Carve-down → temples |

This timeline — roughly 35-40 years for the full complex — aligns well with the actual construction period spanning Khufu's reign (~2589-2566 BCE, 23 years), Khafre's reign (~2558-2532 BCE, 26 years), and Menkaure's reign (~2532-2503 BCE, 29 years). The overlap between reigns provides the overlap between phases.

## Workforce: Builders and Carvers

The two-phase nature of the theory creates two distinct labor pools:

### Builders (Build-Up Phase)

- **Quarry workers**: Cutting blocks from bedrock using copper chisels, wooden wedges, and dolerite pounders. ~5,000-8,000 workers.
- **Haulers**: Sledge teams of 20-40 men per 2.5-ton block. Dragging blocks from quarry to ramp foot, then up the integrated ramps to the working course. ~10,000-15,000 workers at peak.
- **Placers**: Setting blocks on the current course. Leveling, aligning, mortaring. ~2,000-3,000 skilled workers.
- **Ramp builders**: Extending and maintaining the integrated ramp surfaces as the structure grows. This work is indistinguishable from general block placement — the ramp IS the overbuild. Same crews, same skills.
- **Support**: Water carriers (wetting sand for sledge friction), tool sharpeners, food preparers, scribes. ~3,000-5,000.

**Total build-up workforce**: 20,000-30,000 at peak (matching historical estimates).

### Carvers (Carve-Down Phase)

- **Survey masons**: The elite. They establish and maintain the reference lines that define the true pyramid faces. Using plumb bobs, sighting rods, and string lines from the apex, they mark the cut lines on each course of the overbuild. Perhaps 50-100 specialists.
- **Stone cutters**: Working top-down along the marked lines, they cut the overbuild blocks free from the face. This is NOT rough quarry work — they are cutting along pre-marked lines to produce reusable blocks of known dimensions. ~2,000-4,000 skilled masons.
- **Block handlers (descent)**: Moving freed blocks down the remaining ramp surfaces to staging areas. Gravity-assisted — blocks descend ramps rather than climb them, so smaller teams suffice. Brake crews rather than haul crews. ~3,000-5,000 workers.
- **Transport**: Moving blocks from the Great Pyramid staging area to Khafre's site (~450 meters south). Short-haul sledge teams or roller crews. ~2,000-3,000 workers.

**Total carve-down workforce**: 7,000-12,000.

### Simultaneous Operations

A critical operational advantage: **build-up and carve-down can run simultaneously on different faces of the same structure**, and on different structures entirely.

During years 14-16, the Great Pyramid's north and east faces may be undergoing carve-down while the south and west faces are still receiving build-up blocks for the final upper courses. The two workforces do not interfere with each other because they occupy different faces and move material in opposite directions.

By year 16-20, the Great Pyramid is fully in carve-down mode while Khafre's site is in build-up mode. The material stream flows directly from one to the other. The kingdom's total construction workforce remains at roughly 25,000-30,000 — but they are split across two active sites working in complementary phases.

This solves a workforce management problem that plagues other theories: what do you do with 30,000 trained construction workers when the Great Pyramid is finished and Khafre has not yet started? Under Theory E, there is no gap. The carve-down workforce transitions into the build-up workforce as the emphasis shifts from one structure to the next.

## Logistics: Bidirectional Material Flow

Theory E creates a logistics problem unlike any other theory: **material flows in two directions simultaneously**.

### Upbound Stream

Fresh stone from the Giza quarry and Tura travels UP the integrated ramps to the working courses. This is the traditional pyramid logistics challenge — getting 340 blocks per day from quarry face to placement point at increasing heights.

### Downbound Stream

Carved overbuild stone travels DOWN the remaining ramp surfaces to staging areas at the base. This is a new logistics stream that must be coordinated with the upbound flow.

### Deconfliction

The bidirectional flow is manageable because:

1. **Different faces**: During the overlap period, upbound and downbound traffic can use different faces of the trapezoid. Build-up traffic climbs the south face while carve-down material descends the north face.
2. **Time-division**: During dedicated carve-down phases, all ramp surfaces carry downbound traffic. No upbound deconfliction needed.
3. **Natural gradient advantage**: Downbound transport is faster and requires fewer workers per block. Gravity does half the work. A block that took a 40-man team to haul up may need only a 10-man brake crew to lower.

### The Material Processing Advantage

Overbuild stone arriving at Khafre's site has a significant advantage over fresh quarry stone: **it is already cut to standard block dimensions**. The quarry-to-placement pipeline for fresh stone involves:

1. Quarrying (cutting from bedrock)
2. Rough shaping
3. Transport to site
4. Fine dressing
5. Placement

Overbuild stone skips steps 1-2 entirely. It was quarried, shaped, transported, and placed years earlier during the Great Pyramid's build-up. When carved from the overbuild, it arrives at the downstream site as a pre-processed block needing only steps 4-5. This is faster and requires less skilled quarry labor.

Is it easier to "quarry" already-placed stone than to quarry raw bedrock? In several respects, yes:

- **No overburden removal**: Raw quarry faces require removing unusable surface stone to reach quality material. Overbuild stone is already quality material — it passed inspection when it was first placed.
- **Known dimensions**: Overbuild blocks were placed to a standard size. Cutting them free produces blocks of predictable dimensions. Quarry stone is variable.
- **Controlled work environment**: Carvers work on a stable, elevated platform with good sightlines. Quarry workers labor in deepening pits with drainage and access problems.
- **No wedge-splitting gamble**: Raw quarrying with wooden wedges has a failure rate — some blocks crack unpredictably along fossil inclusions in the nummulitic limestone. Overbuild blocks already survived this selection process.

The disadvantage: overbuild blocks may need their mortar joints cleaned, and some may crack during removal. The recovery rate is probably 85-95% — the rest becomes rubble fill or aggregate.

## The Bent Pyramid Connection

The Bent Pyramid at Dahshur (Sneferu, ~2600 BCE) changes angle from 54°27' to 43°22' roughly halfway up. The standard explanation is structural failure — the steep angle was causing internal cracking, and the builders reduced the angle as an emergency measure.

Theory E offers an alternative reading. What if the angle change represents the **discovery** of the overbuild approach?

Consider: the builders start at 54°, which produces a tall, narrow pyramid. Partway up, they realize (perhaps through the Meidum collapse experience) that the steep external faces are difficult to build and prone to failure. They shift to 43° — a much broader profile — and in doing so, create a structure with substantially more mass relative to its contained volume.

The lower section at 54° does not have enough surrounding mass to serve as useful overbuild. The upper section at 43° has generous excess mass on all sides. If someone standing on the Bent Pyramid's upper courses realized that the excess stone surrounding the ideal pyramid shape could be systematically removed AND reused, they might have discovered the accretion overbuild principle empirically.

The Red Pyramid (Sneferu's next project, also at Dahshur, 43°22' throughout) could then be read as the first intentional application of this insight: build broad, build heavy, plan from the start to carve down. The Red Pyramid's consistent 43° angle — notably shallow for a royal pyramid — gives it a generous overbuild profile if you imagine a steeper final form inside it.

By the time Khufu's architects plan Giza, the approach is mature. The Great Pyramid's 51°50' angle represents the optimized balance: steep enough to be imposing, broad enough to support the integrated ramp system, with an overbuild profile calibrated to feed the downstream cascade.

This reading is speculative. But it turns the Bent Pyramid from a mistake into a prototype — which is more consistent with how engineering innovations actually develop.

## Multiple Simultaneous Pyramids

One of the persistent puzzles of the Giza complex is evidence suggesting multiple structures under construction simultaneously. Theory E does not just permit this — it **requires** it.

The cascade only works if the downstream structure is actively absorbing material when the upstream structure begins carve-down. If Khafre's site sits idle while the Great Pyramid's overbuild is removed, the carved stone must be stockpiled somewhere — defeating the logistical elegance.

The optimal schedule has two or three structures in different phases at all times:

- **Structure A**: Carve-down phase (producing material)
- **Structure B**: Build-up phase (consuming material from A + fresh quarry stone)
- **Structure C**: Site preparation / foundation (consuming stockpiled material, light demand)

This creates a rolling wave of construction activity across the plateau, with the workforce shifting its center of gravity from one site to the next over the decades. At any given time, the complex is a landscape of structures in various states: one mostly finished (smooth faces emerging from the top down), one at maximum trapezoid bulk (a stepped mountain of stone), one in early build-up (a broad low platform).

## Maintaining Survey Precision

The final pyramid surfaces must be precise. The Great Pyramid's base is level to within 2.1 centimeters across 230 meters. Its faces are aligned to true north within 3/60 of a degree. How do you achieve this precision when the true faces are buried inside a rough trapezoid until the carve-down?

### Reference Systems

1. **Corner markers**: The four corners of the final pyramid base are established during the foundation survey, using stellar observations for north alignment and water-leveling for the base plane. These points are marked on bedrock and preserved throughout construction. They are the ground-truth reference for all subsequent measurements.

2. **Embedded course markers**: At regular intervals (perhaps every 10-20 courses), reference stones are placed at the exact position where the final pyramid face will intersect that course. These stones are marked with surveyor's notches or paint lines and deliberately placed to be findable during carve-down. They serve as checkpoints: when the carvers reach an embedded marker, they can verify their cut plane matches the planned face.

3. **Apex reference**: The pyramidion (capstone) is set first during carve-down preparation — it defines the apex point. From this single point, plumb lines and string lines can be dropped to the corner markers, establishing the four face planes geometrically.

4. **Water leveling**: Channels cut along each course and flooded with water establish perfect horizontals — the same technique used for the foundation, applied at each level during carve-down.

### Top-Down Precision

Working from the top down has a geometric advantage for precision: each newly cut course can be verified against the courses already completed above it and against the string lines connecting apex to base corners. Errors are caught immediately and corrected on the next course. If you build faces bottom-up (as in conventional theories), an error at course 50 propagates upward through 150 more courses before anyone sees the accumulated deviation.

The carve-down approach effectively turns pyramid face construction into a **surveying problem** rather than a **stacking problem**. The masons are sculptors revealing a form from a rough mass, guided by reference geometry — much like a sculptor working from a maquette, but with precisely placed internal reference points.

## Time Estimates at Great Pyramid Scale

### Build-Up Phase: 14-16 Years

- **Total trapezoid mass**: ~8.5 million tons
- **Working days per year**: ~300 (accounting for festivals, rest days, extreme weather)
- **Blocks per day (average)**: ~400-450 (higher than the standard 340 estimate because ramp material counts as placed blocks, not overhead)
- **Peak daily rate (years 3-10)**: ~500-600 blocks, leveraging the broad lower ramp system and multiple parallel sledge tracks
- **Final years rate (years 12-16)**: ~150-200 blocks, limited by narrow upper ramps

### Carve-Down Phase: 4-8 Years

- **Total material removed**: ~2.4 million tons
- **Blocks removed per day**: ~200-300 (faster per-block than placement because gravity assists and the stone is pre-cut)
- **Overlap with build-up**: The carve-down begins ~2 years before the build-up finishes, starting at the apex while the last upper courses are placed
- **Overlap with Khafre build-up**: Carved material begins flowing to Khafre's site as soon as the carve-down generates consistent output (~year 15-16)

### Total Great Pyramid Timeline: ~18-22 Years

This matches the conventional estimate of ~20 years for the Great Pyramid's construction. The difference from other theories is not the total time — it is the character of the work in each phase and the productive output of the carve-down.

## Why Theory E Creates the Best Game Mechanics

Every other construction theory produces a game that looks like this: choose a quarry, build a supply chain, deliver blocks to one site until it is done, repeat for the next site. Hub-and-spoke, one project at a time, same problem three times.

Theory E produces a game that looks like this:

### Economic Web

Every structure is both a consumer and a producer. The player must manage **material flow between structures**, not just from quarry to site. This creates a supply web with feedback loops, backpressure, and cascading effects. If the Great Pyramid's carve-down runs ahead of Khafre's build-up capacity, overbuild material piles up in staging areas. If Khafre's build-up absorbs faster than the carve-down produces, the workforce idles waiting for supply.

The player is balancing a **pipeline**, not running a batch job.

### Material Recycling

No other theory gives the player a reason to care about what happens to construction material after it is placed. Under Theory E, every block you place during build-up is a block you (or your successor) will later remove and send downstream. Block quality matters twice: once for structural integrity of the overbuild, and again for reusability when carved free. The player thinks about the full lifecycle of every stone.

### Multi-Site Parallelism

The DAG structure creates natural multi-site gameplay. The player is not managing one big project and then another big project. They are managing a **complex of interdependent projects** at different phases, each with different labor needs, material flows, and bottlenecks. This is the logistics game at its richest — it is Factorio's production chains applied to ancient construction.

### Phase Transitions as Gameplay Events

The shift from build-up to carve-down is not a cutscene. It is a **workforce retooling event**. Haulers become brake crews. Unskilled labor is partially released. Skilled masons are recruited or promoted. The supply chain reverses direction. The player must manage this transition without losing throughput — on either the carve-down output or the downstream build-up input.

### Overbuild Decisions Propagate

How much overbuild you design into the Great Pyramid is not a fixed number — it is a **player decision**. More overbuild means:
- Wider, more gradual ramps (easier build-up, higher throughput)
- More material to carve (longer carve-down phase)
- More material feeding Khafre (less fresh quarry stone needed downstream)
- A bigger, more visible trapezoid (more workforce, more food, more logistics)

Less overbuild means:
- Narrower ramps (harder build-up, skilled labor bottleneck)
- Less carving (shorter carve-down, faster to see the finished pyramid)
- Less cascade material (more quarry pressure on downstream projects)
- A leaner project (fewer workers, lower overhead)

This is a genuine strategic trade-off that propagates through the entire Giza complex DAG. The player's overbuild ratio for the Great Pyramid shapes the logistics of Menkaure's temple construction decades later. That kind of long-range consequence is the hallmark of a deep strategy game.

### The Satisfaction of Reveal

And there is one more thing — an aesthetic mechanic that no other theory provides. The carve-down is a **reveal**. The player watches their rough, blocky trapezoid transform into a gleaming pyramid as the masons work their way down the faces. Smooth white Tura limestone casing emerges from the rubble-textured overbuild, course by course, from apex to base. After years of building an ugly mass, the beautiful thing inside it is unveiled.

That is a moment worth engineering a game around.
