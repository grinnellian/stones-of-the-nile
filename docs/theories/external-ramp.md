# External Ramp Theory: Operational Deep Dive

Research output for game simulation. All numbers scaled to the Great Pyramid at Giza (230.3m base, 146.6m height, ~2.3 million blocks, ~2.6 million cubic meters of stone) unless otherwise noted.

---

## 1. Physical Description: Ramp Geometry

Three principal variants exist. Each solves one problem while creating others.

### 1a. Straight (Linear) Ramp

A single inclined causeway running from ground level to the current working course of the pyramid, perpendicular to one face.

- **Slope**: 7-8% gradient (roughly 1:12 to 1:14). This is the maximum practical slope for teams hauling 2.5-ton blocks on sledges. Steeper gradients (up to 10-12%) are possible for lighter loads or with more haulers, but the energy cost per block rises sharply.
- **Width**: 15-20m at the base for a single-lane ramp (one sledge at a time), 30-40m for a dual-lane configuration (one up, one down). Side walls or raised edges add 1-2m on each side.
- **Length at full pyramid height**: At 8% gradient, reaching 146.6m requires approximately 1,830m of ramp (nearly 2km). At 7%, approximately 2,100m.
- **Cross-section**: Trapezoidal. The ramp narrows slightly toward the top. Interior is rubble fill; surface is a compacted layer of tafla clay or gypsum-sealed mudbrick, kept smooth and wet for sledge friction.
- **Side walls**: Mudbrick retaining walls, 1-2m thick, to contain the rubble fill and prevent lateral collapse.

**The fatal problem**: A ramp 2km long, 20m wide, and tapering from 0 to 146.6m in height contains roughly 2.7 million cubic meters of material -- more than the pyramid itself. You are building two monuments, and the temporary one is bigger.

### 1b. Zigzag (Reversing/Switchback) Ramp

The ramp runs parallel to one face of the pyramid, hits the corner, reverses direction along the adjacent face, and continues upward in switchbacks. Each run is roughly the length of one pyramid face (~230m for the lower courses, shrinking as the pyramid narrows).

- **Slope**: Same 7-8% gradient per run.
- **Width**: 10-15m per lane. Space is tighter because the ramp hugs the pyramid face.
- **Total ramp length (path)**: Comparable to the straight ramp in total distance traveled, but folded into a compact footprint.
- **Height gain per run**: At 8% slope over 230m, each run gains ~18.4m of elevation. Reaching 146.6m requires roughly 8 reversals.
- **Turning platforms**: At each reversal, a flat platform 15-25m long is needed for sledge teams to negotiate the 180-degree turn. This is operationally expensive -- a loaded sledge with 30+ haulers cannot turn sharply. The team must lever-rotate the sledge or transfer the block to a fresh sledge oriented the other way.

**Advantage**: The ramp footprint stays adjacent to the pyramid rather than stretching across the desert. Total ramp volume is reduced by perhaps 30-40% compared to a straight ramp, because the ramp can lean against the pyramid face (using the pyramid itself as one retaining wall).

**Problem**: Turning platforms are throughput bottlenecks. Every reversal point is a potential traffic jam. Block delivery rate is governed by the slowest turn, not the hauling speed.

### 1c. Spiral (Wrap-Around) Ramp

The ramp wraps around all four faces of the pyramid, spiraling upward like a helical road.

- **Slope**: 7-8% gradient, same constraint.
- **Width**: 8-12m. Narrower than the other variants because it must wrap around the pyramid without projecting too far.
- **Perimeter per circuit**: At the base, one full circuit is ~920m (4 x 230m). Each circuit gains ~73.6m of elevation at 8% slope. So reaching 146.6m requires roughly 2 full circuits at the base level. But as the pyramid narrows, each circuit is shorter, and the ramp steepens or must add more circuits.
- **Corner turns**: 90-degree turns at each corner, four per circuit. Less severe than the zigzag's 180-degree reversal, but still a throughput constraint. Loaded sledges need a turning radius of 5-8m minimum.

**Advantage**: Smallest footprint. The ramp clings to the pyramid structure. Total ramp volume is perhaps 50-60% less than a straight ramp.

**Problems**: (1) The ramp obscures all four faces of the pyramid during construction, making it impossible to check alignment and corner angles from a distance -- a serious surveying problem for a structure that achieves 0.05-degree accuracy. (2) The ramp gets progressively narrower and steeper as the pyramid tapers. Above ~100m, the available face width is less than 60m per side, and the ramp may be too narrow for practical sledge hauling. (3) The ramp must be structurally bonded to or leaning against the pyramid face, raising questions about damage during dismantling.

## 2. Materials: What the Ramp Is Made Of

The ramp is a construction project in its own right, consuming enormous quantities of material.

### Core fill
- **Limestone chips and rubble**: The primary fill material. Quarrying operations for the pyramid produce substantial waste (estimated 10-30% of quarried volume becomes chips and unusable fragments). This is recycled into ramp fill.
- **Desert sand and gravel**: Locally abundant, low effort to gather. Compacts reasonably well. Poor structural integrity on its own.
- **Tafla**: A local clay-like desert sediment found around Giza. Compacts hard when wetted and dried. Used as a binder mixed with rubble.

### Structure
- **Mudbrick retaining walls**: The sides of the ramp are built up in courses of mudbrick (Nile mud + straw, sun-dried). Standard Egyptian mudbrick dimensions: roughly 30 x 15 x 8 cm. The retaining walls must be 1-2m thick to contain fill at the ramp's lower (wider, taller) sections.
- **Cross-timbers**: Wooden beams laid across the ramp width at intervals (every 5-10m) to prevent lateral spreading. Wood is expensive and imported -- this is a real resource cost.

### Surface
- **Compacted tafla or gypsum-mortared mudbrick**: The hauling surface must be smooth, hard, and capable of being wetted. A rough surface dramatically increases sledge friction and hauling labor.
- **Timber sleepers**: Some reconstructions include transverse timber sleepers (like railroad ties) embedded in the ramp surface to provide a consistent, low-friction track for sledge runners. This requires substantial wood.

### Resource estimates for a straight ramp to full pyramid height (~2.7M cubic meters)
- **Mudbrick**: 2-4 million bricks for retaining walls alone (each ~3.6 liters, so 7,200-14,400 cubic meters of brick). Manufacturing requires mud, straw, water, drying time, labor.
- **Rubble fill**: ~2.5 million cubic meters. Density ~1,800 kg/m3, so ~4.5 million tons of fill material.
- **Wood**: 500-2,000 structural timbers for cross-bracing. Imported cedar or local acacia. Each timber 3-5m long, 15-20cm diameter.
- **Water**: Continuous supply for surface wetting during use. A wetted surface must be re-wetted every 15-30 minutes in Egyptian summer heat (40+ degrees C). Estimated 2,000-5,000 liters/day for the active hauling surface.

## 3. Construction Sequence: How the Ramp Grows with the Pyramid

This is the operational heart of the theory. The ramp and pyramid are built simultaneously, each constraining the other.

### Phase 1: Foundation and Lower Courses (0-30m, Years 1-5)

- **Ramp**: A modest ramp, 300-400m long (at 8% gradient to reach 30m), 20m wide. Volume: ~90,000 cubic meters. Easily manageable. At this stage the ramp is a minor construction task compared to the pyramid.
- **Pyramid**: The first 30m of height comprises roughly 70% of the pyramid's total volume (~1.8 million cubic meters). This is the high-throughput phase. Courses are long (230m per side), blocks are numerous per course, and the hauling distance is short.
- **Block delivery rate**: Peak rate. The ramp is short, the work face is wide, many blocks can be placed simultaneously across the course. Estimated 500-800 blocks/day is achievable during this phase with full workforce.
- **Ramp builders vs pyramid builders**: Ramp construction is perhaps 5-10% of total labor effort. One or two dedicated divisions (40-100 men) can keep the ramp growing ahead of the pyramid.

### Phase 2: Middle Courses (30-90m, Years 5-12)

- **Ramp**: Now 375-1,125m long. Volume growing rapidly -- by the time the ramp reaches 90m, a straight ramp contains ~900,000 cubic meters of material. The ramp is now a major logistics operation.
- **Pyramid**: Each course is smaller (the pyramid narrows), so fewer blocks per course. But the ramp is longer, so each block takes longer to deliver. Throughput begins to decline.
- **Critical transition (~60m)**: Around this height, a straight ramp's volume exceeds 400,000 cubic meters, and the daily material requirement for ramp expansion starts to compete seriously with pyramid block delivery. The ramp needs its own supply chain.
- **Ramp workforce grows**: 10-20% of labor effort is now devoted to ramp construction and maintenance. Dedicated ramp-building crews of 500-2,000 men.
- **Hauling time increases**: At 60m, haulers walk 750m uphill per block (at 8% gradient). Round-trip time per sledge team: 2-3 hours including loading, hauling, placement, and return. This limits throughput to perhaps 5-8 blocks per sledge team per day.

### Phase 3: Upper Courses (90-146.6m, Years 12-20)

- **Ramp**: A straight ramp to 146.6m is 1,830m long and contains ~2.7 million cubic meters. The ramp is now AS LARGE AS THE PYRAMID. This is the central absurdity of the straight ramp theory.
- **Pyramid**: The upper 30% of pyramid height contains only ~3% of the pyramid's volume (~78,000 cubic meters, roughly 31,000 blocks). But delivering each block requires a nearly 2km uphill sledge haul.
- **Round-trip time**: 4-6 hours per block at the final height. Even with multiple sledge teams operating simultaneously, throughput drops to 50-100 blocks per day.
- **The switchover**: More workers are building/maintaining the ramp than building the pyramid. The project has inverted. The ramp IS the project; the pyramid is a side effect.

### The Impracticality Threshold

Most Egyptologists who accept external ramps at all argue they were used only for the lower portion, typically the bottom 30-50m (where 60-80% of the pyramid's mass lives). Above that, another method -- levers, an internal ramp, or some unknown technique -- would take over. This is the "hybrid theory" approach.

The critical heights:
- **30m**: Ramp is still clearly manageable. ~90,000 m3 of fill. No controversy.
- **60m**: Ramp is large but feasible. ~400,000 m3. Starting to strain resources.
- **90m**: Ramp is enormous. ~900,000-1,200,000 m3. Requires dedicated supply chain rivaling the pyramid itself.
- **120m**: Ramp volume exceeds pyramid volume remaining to build. The economics have flipped.
- **146.6m**: Ramp volume equals or exceeds total pyramid volume. Operationally absurd for a straight ramp.

## 4. Workforce

### Total workforce for the ramp-based approach (Great Pyramid scale)

**Peak total**: 25,000-30,000 during inundation season (Akhet). **Off-season minimum**: 8,000-10,000 (permanent skilled workers + retained haulers).

### Roles and estimated headcounts (peak season, mid-construction)

| Role | Headcount | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Block haulers** | 8,000-12,000 | Organized in sledge teams of 20-40 men per 2.5t block. 200-400 active sledge teams. |
| **Water pourers** | 500-1,000 | Stationed every 50-100m along the ramp surface. Pour water ahead of the sledge to reduce friction by ~50%. 2-4 men per station, carrying water in ceramic jars from cisterns at ramp base and intermediate points. |
| **Ramp builders** | 2,000-4,000 | Build and extend the ramp as the pyramid grows. Mudbrick makers, rubble haulers, tafla compactors, retaining wall masons. This headcount grows as the ramp grows. |
| **Quarry workers** | 3,000-5,000 | Cutters, wedge-drivers, rough-shapers. Work at local Giza quarry (core blocks) and Tura (casing). Not on the ramp, but their output feeds the ramp's hauling teams. |
| **Block setters** | 1,000-2,000 | Skilled masons at the pyramid work face. Receive blocks from haulers, lever them into final position, check fit, apply mortar. The bottleneck is often HERE, not on the ramp. |
| **Lever/crowbar teams** | 500-1,000 | At the ramp summit, transfer blocks from the ramp onto the current course. Use wooden levers (3-4m acacia or cedar poles) and copper-tipped crowbars. Also handle fine positioning. |
| **Supply workers** | 2,000-3,000 | Food, water, tool delivery. Bakers, brewers, water carriers, copper-tool sharpeners. Support the entire site. |
| **Overseers/scribes** | 200-500 | Crew leaders, phyle commanders, record-keepers. The management layer. |
| **Rope/tool makers** | 300-500 | Continuous consumption of rope (palm fiber, papyrus) and copper tools. Rope wears out in days under heavy hauling use. |

### Workforce scaling problem

The hauler headcount is roughly constant (governed by throughput target), but the ramp-builder headcount scales with ramp size. In Phase 1, 100 ramp builders suffice. By Phase 3, 4,000+ ramp builders are needed, and they compete for the same food, water, housing, and management attention as the pyramid builders.

## 5. Logistics: Competing Supply Chains

This is where the external ramp theory creates its most interesting gameplay dynamics. Two supply chains compete for overlapping resources.

### Pyramid supply chain (limestone blocks)
1. **Quarry** (Giza local or Tura) --> rough-cut blocks, 2.5t average
2. **Sledge transport** to ramp base (Giza quarry: 300-500m; Tura: by barge to harbor, then 1-2km overland)
3. **Ramp ascent** via sledge teams (length depends on current height)
4. **Placement** on current course by lever/setter teams

### Ramp supply chain (fill materials)
1. **Rubble collection** from quarry waste, desert surface, demolition of older structures
2. **Mudbrick production** at brickworks near the Nile (needs mud, straw, water, drying yards)
3. **Transport** of fill to ramp extension point (the leading edge of the ramp, which is always the highest and most remote point)
4. **Compaction and surfacing** -- tafla or gypsum layer on top, retaining walls on sides

### Resource conflicts

| Resource | Pyramid needs | Ramp needs | Conflict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Labor** | Haulers, setters, quarriers | Mudbrick makers, rubble haulers, compactors | Both draw from the same seasonal conscript pool. Every ramp builder is a hauler you don't have. |
| **Water** | Sledge lubrication, mortar mixing, worker drinking | Mudbrick production, tafla compaction, surface wetting | Water must be carried from the Nile (1-2km). Water carriers serve both chains. In drought years, both suffer. |
| **Wood** | Sledges (~50-100 active sledges, each lasting weeks), levers, rollers | Cross-timbers for ramp bracing, timber sleepers for ramp surface | Imported cedar is the bottleneck resource. Sledge replacement alone requires continuous timber supply. |
| **Copper** | Quarry tools (chisels, saws) | Minimal (retaining walls are mudbrick, not cut stone) | Minor conflict. Ramp is low-tech compared to pyramid. |
| **Management** | Scribes tracking block production, delivery, placement | Scribes tracking fill material, ramp progress, surface quality | Oversight bandwidth is finite. The ramp adds a parallel project requiring its own management hierarchy. |
| **Space** | Staging areas at ramp base and summit | Ramp footprint occupies ground that could be staging area | A straight ramp nearly 2km long occupies enormous ground area. Zigzag and spiral variants reduce this but create access problems. |

### The fundamental tension for gameplay

The ramp supply chain starts trivial and ends dominant. In years 1-5, it is an afterthought -- a small crew extending a small ramp. By years 12-20, the ramp supply chain consumes as much or more labor, material, and management capacity as the pyramid itself. The player must anticipate this crossover and plan for it, or face a mid-project crisis where the ramp starves the pyramid of resources.

## 6. Transport Mechanics

### Sledge hauling (primary method)

- **Sledge design**: Wooden runners, 2-4m long, 1-1.5m wide. Flat bed for the block. Ropes attached at the front (hauling) and rear (braking on descent).
- **Team size**: 20-40 men for a 2.5-ton block on a 7-8% grade. Calculation: coefficient of friction on wetted surface ~0.2-0.3; on a 7% grade the effective pull force needed is roughly 0.27 x 2,500kg x 9.8 = ~6,600N. Each man can sustain ~150-200N of pulling force. So 33-44 men. Round to 30-40 for practical team size with rotation.
- **Speed**: 0.5-1.0 km/h uphill under load. Walking pace is ~5 km/h, so the loaded sledge moves at 10-20% of walking speed.
- **Rope**: 40-60mm diameter papyrus or palm-fiber rope, 50-100m long. Multiple ropes per sledge (2-4 hauling ropes, 1-2 braking ropes). Rope is a consumable -- heavy hauling use wears through a rope in 3-7 days.

### Water lubrication

- **Method**: 1-2 men walk 5-10m ahead of the sledge, pouring water from ceramic jars onto the compacted surface. The water creates a thin film that reduces friction by approximately 50% (confirmed by Fall et al., 2014, University of Amsterdam).
- **Quantity**: Approximately 2-3 liters per meter of ramp surface per pass. For a 1,000m ramp, each sledge passage requires ~2,000-3,000 liters of water.
- **Evaporation rate**: In Egyptian summer (40+ C, low humidity), the wetted surface dries in 15-30 minutes. Continuous re-wetting is needed if multiple sledges use the ramp in sequence.
- **Water supply infrastructure**: Ceramic jars (10-20 liters each) filled at cisterns positioned at intervals along the ramp. Cisterns fed by water carriers from the Nile. At peak operation, 100+ water carriers may be dedicated to ramp lubrication alone.

### Rollers (supplementary)

- **Use case**: Primarily at the ramp base for moving blocks from the staging area onto the ramp surface, and at the summit for moving blocks off the ramp onto the pyramid course.
- **Design**: Cylindrical wooden logs, 15-20cm diameter, 1-2m long. Placed transversely under the sledge.
- **Problem**: Rollers work on flat or gentle grades but are dangerous on slopes -- they can roll backward under the load. Unlikely to be used on the ramp incline itself.

### Lever systems at the summit

The transition from ramp to pyramid course is a critical operational chokepoint.

- **Method**: The block arrives at the ramp summit on its sledge. Lever teams (4-8 men with wooden levers, 3-4m long) lift one end of the block, insert packing stones underneath, then lever the other end. By alternately levering each end, the block is "walked" sideways off the ramp and onto the pyramid course.
- **Time per block**: 30-60 minutes for the summit transfer, depending on precision requirements.
- **Alternative**: If the ramp is built to overshoot the current course by a meter or so, blocks can be sledged directly off the ramp onto the course without levering. But this requires continuous ramp height adjustment.

### Heavy blocks (granite beams, 25-80 tons)

The King's Chamber ceiling beams and relieving chamber beams weigh 25-80 tons each, requiring fundamentally different handling:

- **Hauling teams**: 200-800 men per block (scaling linearly from the 2.5-ton calculation).
- **Ramp width**: The ramp must be wide enough for a single sledge carrying an 80-ton block (2-3m wide, 5-7m long). Minimum ramp width of 12-15m for safe passage.
- **Ramp strength**: The concentrated load of an 80-ton block on a sledge exerts enormous pressure on the ramp surface. The surface must be reinforced with timber planking or stone pavers at these loads. Ramp failure (surface collapse, retaining wall blowout) under heavy loads is a real risk.
- **Speed**: 0.1-0.3 km/h. A single 80-ton granite beam might take an entire day to haul from the base to the 60m level where the King's Chamber sits.

## 7. The Last 30% Problem

This is the theory's defining weakness and the most interesting design space for the game.

### The mathematics

The Great Pyramid is approximately a square pyramid with base 230.3m and height 146.6m.

| Height reached | % of total height | % of total volume below this height | Ramp volume (straight, 8%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30m | 20% | ~70% | ~90,000 m3 |
| 60m | 41% | ~90% | ~360,000 m3 |
| 90m | 61% | ~97% | ~810,000 m3 |
| 120m | 82% | ~99.4% | ~1,440,000 m3 |
| 146.6m | 100% | 100% | ~2,700,000 m3 |

The pyramid's volume is ~2,600,000 m3. So the ramp to full height is LARGER than the pyramid.

But notice: the last 30% of height (top 44m) contains only ~3% of the pyramid's volume -- roughly 78,000 m3 or ~31,000 blocks. Yet reaching this height requires a ramp of 2.7 million cubic meters (if straight).

The ratio of ramp volume to remaining pyramid volume becomes absurd:
- At 90m height: 810,000 m3 of ramp to deliver 78,000 m3 of pyramid. A 10:1 ratio.
- At 120m height: 1,440,000 m3 of ramp to deliver 15,600 m3 of pyramid. A 92:1 ratio.

### Operational consequences

- **Time**: In Phase 3, each block requires a 4-6 hour round trip. With 200 active sledge teams (the physical limit given ramp width and spacing), throughput is 50-100 blocks per day. The remaining ~31,000 blocks take 300-600 working days, or roughly 1.5-3 years at the standard 250 working days/year.
- **Ramp maintenance**: A 2km ramp in the Egyptian desert degrades continuously. Wind erosion, heat cracking of the tafla surface, mudbrick retaining wall decay from thermal cycling. A dedicated maintenance crew of 500-1,000 is needed just to keep the ramp functional.
- **The capstone problem**: The pyramidion (capstone) sits at the very apex. It is a single block, perhaps 1-2 tons, but it must be delivered to 146.6m. This is the culmination of the entire ramp infrastructure, and it delivers one block.

### How builders may have coped

Several strategies reduce (but do not eliminate) the scaling problem:

1. **Shrink the ramp as the pyramid narrows**: Above ~60m, the pyramid face is significantly narrower. A zigzag or spiral ramp can be narrowed correspondingly, since the blocks are smaller and fewer per course.
2. **Steepen the gradient for upper sections**: The final blocks are lighter (smaller courses near the apex). A 10-12% gradient might be tolerable for blocks under 1 ton, reducing ramp length.
3. **Switch methods above a threshold**: Use the external ramp for the bottom 30-60m, then switch to levers, an internal ramp, or a crane-like device for the upper courses. This is the most commonly accepted hybrid approach.
4. **Use the pyramid steps as the ramp**: If the pyramid is built in stepped accretion layers (as in Theory E), the steps themselves can serve as crude ramps or lever platforms, eliminating the need for a separate external ramp above a certain height.

## 8. Dismantling the Ramp

The ramp is temporary. After the pyramid is complete, the ramp must be removed. This is itself a major operation that is often overlooked.

### Timeline
- Dismantling proceeds top-down. The ramp is removed in reverse, starting from the summit.
- As each section of ramp is removed, the casing stones for that level of the pyramid are set in place (casing was likely installed top-down on the external faces).
- Estimated dismantling time: 2-5 years for a full-height straight ramp, working with 2,000-4,000 laborers.

### Material disposition
- **Mudbrick**: Degrades quickly when exposed. Largely abandoned or dissolved by rain/wind. Some recycled into secondary structures (workers' village walls, temporary buildings).
- **Rubble and limestone chips**: Spread across the plateau. Some of the rubble fields around the Giza pyramids may be ramp remnants.
- **Wood**: Recovered and reused. Timber is too valuable to discard. Cross-timbers, sleepers, and retaining-wall headers would be salvaged.
- **Tafla**: Left in place or leveled. Becomes part of the plateau surface.

### Archaeological visibility
This is why external ramp evidence is sparse: the ramp was designed to be temporary and its materials were either perishable (mudbrick, wood) or indistinguishable from general construction debris (rubble, sand, chips). A demolished ramp looks like a messy construction site, not a preserved structure. Some traces have been identified at Meidum and at the Sinki pyramid.

### For the game
Ramp dismantling represents a significant post-construction labor and time commitment. The pyramid is "structurally complete" before it is "visually complete" -- the casing stones that make it gleam white are installed during the dismantling phase, working top-down. This creates a two-phase completion: structural completion (last core block placed) and aesthetic completion (last casing block placed, last section of ramp removed).

## 9. Time Estimates at Great Pyramid Scale

All estimates assume a straight ramp approach used exclusively (the maximally difficult case).

### Phase breakdown

| Phase | Duration | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Site preparation** | 1-2 years | Level the plateau (to within 2cm across 230m -- extraordinary precision). Build workers' village. Establish quarry operations. Construct harbor and causeway from Nile. Build initial ramp foundation. |
| **Lower pyramid (0-30m)** | 3-5 years | ~70% of pyramid volume placed. Ramp is short, throughput is high. 500-800 blocks/day at peak. Ramp requires minimal dedicated labor. |
| **Middle pyramid (30-90m)** | 4-6 years | ~27% of volume. Throughput declining as ramp lengthens. Ramp expansion becomes a parallel project requiring dedicated crews. 200-400 blocks/day. |
| **Upper pyramid (90-146.6m)** | 3-5 years | ~3% of volume, but each block takes 4-6 hours round trip. 50-100 blocks/day. Ramp maintenance is a constant drain. |
| **Casing and ramp removal** | 2-5 years | Install casing stones top-down. Dismantle ramp top-down. Finish site cleanup. |
| **Total** | 13-23 years | Consistent with the historical ~20 year estimate. |

### Daily schedule (peak season, mid-construction)

A working day is roughly 10-12 hours (dawn to dusk minus midday heat break).

- **Dawn (6 AM)**: Water carriers begin wetting the ramp surface. Block staging area receives first blocks from quarry (quarried the previous day or drawn from stockpile).
- **Morning shift (6-11 AM)**: Primary hauling. Sledge teams load, ascend, deliver, descend. Each team completes 1-2 round trips before the midday break. Water pourers active continuously. Block setters receiving and placing.
- **Midday break (11 AM - 1 PM)**: Heat is extreme. Workers rest in shade. Bread and beer distributed. Ramp surface maintenance (patching, re-compaction of damaged sections).
- **Afternoon shift (1-5 PM)**: Second hauling shift. Throughput slightly reduced due to heat. Quarry operations continue. Ramp builders extend the ramp in sections not currently used for hauling.
- **Evening (5-6 PM)**: Final deliveries. Tool collection and sharpening. Crew tallies by scribes. Stockpile accounting.

### Seasonal modulation

- **Akhet (flood, 4 months)**: Peak operations. Full workforce of 25,000-30,000. Maximum block delivery rate. Nile transport at peak (casing stone from Tura, granite from Aswan arriving by barge).
- **Peret (growing, 4 months)**: Workforce drops to 12,000-15,000. Seasonal conscripts return to farms. Construction continues at reduced rate. Quarrying at full capacity (building stockpiles for next Akhet).
- **Shemu (harvest, 4 months)**: Minimum workforce of 8,000-10,000. Permanent skilled workers only. Focus on precision work (block setting, surveying, tool production) rather than volume hauling. Ramp maintenance and extension during this period, preparing for next flood season's construction push.
