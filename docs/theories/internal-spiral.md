# Internal Spiral Ramp Theory (Houdin)

Jean-Pierre Houdin's internal spiral ramp theory, first published in 2007 and developed in collaboration with Dassault Systemes' 3D modeling capabilities, proposes that the Great Pyramid was built using two complementary ramp systems: a conventional external ramp for the lower third and an internal spiral ramp embedded within the pyramid's own masonry for the upper two-thirds. This document describes how the system would have worked operationally.

---

## Phase 1: The External Ramp (Bottom ~30%)

### Geometry and Purpose

The first approximately 43 meters of height (roughly 30% of the final 146.6m) is built using a conventional straight external ramp. At this stage the pyramid's base is so wide (230.3m per side) that the courses are enormous — hundreds of meters of working face — and the height is modest enough that a traditional ramp remains practical.

- **Ramp gradient**: 7-8% (approximately 1:12 to 1:14 slope)
- **Ramp length**: ~550-600m for a straight ramp reaching 43m elevation
- **Ramp width**: 6-8m at the hauling surface, wide enough for two-way sledge traffic
- **Materials**: Tafla clay, limestone rubble and chips, mudbrick fill — the same disposable fill used in all Egyptian ramp construction
- **Hauling surface**: Wetted tafla clay or timber sleepers; water carriers ahead of each sledge reduce friction by roughly 50%

### Operations During Phase 1

This is brute-force hauling, identical to what Egyptians had practiced for generations. Sledge teams of 20-40 men drag 2.5-ton limestone blocks up the ramp. At the base courses, blocks are large and the working face is vast, so multiple sledge teams operate in parallel. Placement crews use levers and rockers to position blocks.

During Phase 1 the workforce is at its largest — perhaps 20,000-25,000 during the inundation season — because the work is unskilled hauling across a broad front. The quarry at the south/west of the plateau is close; turnaround times are short.

The critical detail: **during Phase 1, the internal ramp is already being built into the courses.** The masons leave a continuous corridor spiraling upward within the masonry as each course is laid. The ramp is not cut into the pyramid later — it is constructed course by course, its ceiling formed by the courses above.

### Transition

At approximately 43m, the external ramp is dismantled. Its material is partly recycled as pyramid fill. From this point forward all block transport to the working face uses the internal spiral ramp whose lower entrance is at ground level.

The 43m threshold is not arbitrary. Below this height, the pyramid contains roughly 70-75% of its total mass (because volume scales cubically with base width, and the base courses are far larger than the upper courses). So the external ramp handles the majority of the pyramid's mass but only the first third of its height — exactly the range where external ramps are practical.

## Phase 2: The Internal Spiral Ramp (Upper ~70% of Height)

### Physical Description

The internal ramp is a continuous corridor spiraling upward inside the pyramid's body, making one full circuit (four straight runs connected by four corner turns) per revolution. It climbs at a constant gradient, with each revolution gaining a fixed amount of height.

**Core dimensions:**
- **Width**: approximately 1.5-2.0m (Houdin specifies ~1.6m usable width)
- **Height**: approximately 2.0-2.2m ceiling clearance
- **Gradient**: 7% (1:14), consistent with known Egyptian ramp practice
- **Distance per revolution**: approximately 4 x (current side length at that elevation) — this shrinks as the pyramid narrows
- **Height gained per revolution**: at 7% grade, each revolution gains roughly 7% of the perimeter traversed. At the 43m level, the pyramid face is about 170m per side; one revolution is ~680m of ramp, gaining ~48m. At the 120m level, each side is roughly 40m; one revolution is ~160m, gaining ~11m.
- **Total ramp length**: approximately 1.5-2.0 km from bottom entrance to the summit courses

The ramp runs roughly 10-15m inboard from each face, embedded in the mass of the pyramid but not centered — it stays within the outer structural zone so that the pyramid's core masonry remains an uninterrupted solid mass.

### Cross-Section

The ramp corridor is rectangular in cross-section: flat floor, vertical walls, flat ceiling. The floor, walls, and ceiling are all formed by the pyramid's own masonry courses. The floor is finished smooth for sledge runners. The walls and ceiling are structural — they bear the weight of the courses above.

This is a key point: the ramp is not a void cut through solid stone. It is an intentional corridor left open as the courses are laid, just as the internal passages, the Grand Gallery, and the King's Chamber are intentional voids within the structure. Egyptian masons had generations of experience building corridors and chambers within pyramids by the time of Khufu's project.

### How Blocks Enter the Ramp

Blocks arrive at the ramp's ground-level entrance from the quarry staging area, the same route they used during Phase 1. The entrance is at the pyramid's base, on one face (likely the north or south face closest to the quarry). Blocks are loaded onto wooden sledges and hauled into the ramp mouth by teams of men pulling ropes.

Inside the ramp, the hauling method changes. The corridor is too narrow for a team of 20-40 men to walk abreast pulling a sledge. Instead:

- **Rope teams** work in single file or two abreast ahead of the sledge, extending up the ramp corridor. A team of 12-20 men strings out along 30-50m of ramp.
- **Rollers or greased timber sleepers** reduce friction on the ramp floor. In a 1.6m-wide corridor, a sledge is approximately 0.8-1.0m wide, leaving narrow margins.
- **Relay stations**: men can be stationed along the ramp at intervals, fresh teams taking over as tired teams step into alcoves or passing bays.

### How Blocks Exit onto the Working Face

At each level where blocks are needed, the ramp intersects the current working course. Blocks are pulled off the ramp through openings onto the pyramid's exterior working surface. As the pyramid grows, lower exit points are sealed with masonry — they become permanent parts of the pyramid structure. Only the topmost exit point (at the current working level) is active at any given time.

## Corner Mechanics: The Open Notches

### The Problem

The ramp makes a 90-degree turn at each corner of the pyramid. A 2.5-ton block on a 3m-long sledge cannot turn a sharp corner inside a 1.6m-wide corridor.

### Houdin's Solution: Corner Notches

At each of the four corners, the ramp opens onto a small platform exposed to the outside of the pyramid. These are open notches — gaps in the pyramid's face at the corner, roughly 5-8m wide, creating a flat turning area open to the sky and air.

**How the turn works:**

1. The sledge arrives at the corner platform from the straight run.
2. The platform is large enough (~5m x 5m minimum) for the sledge to be rotated 90 degrees. Workers use levers and a pivoting post (a wooden bollard anchored in the platform floor) to swing the sledge around.
3. The sledge enters the next straight run, now traveling along the adjacent face.

**Physical evidence:** In 2008, Bob Brier and Jean-Pierre Houdin identified what appears to be a notch at the northeast corner of the Great Pyramid at approximately 90m elevation. The notch is a gap in the masonry consistent with a turning platform that was never fully sealed.

**The notches also solve two other problems:**
- **Ventilation**: the open corners allow air circulation through the otherwise enclosed ramp corridor. Stale air exits at the notches; fresh air is drawn in through the base entrance and lower notches.
- **Lighting**: the corner notches admit daylight. Between notches, the straight runs (50-170m depending on elevation) would require oil lamps or reflected sunlight using polished copper mirrors — a technique attested in other Egyptian construction contexts.

### Corner Notches at Different Elevations

As the pyramid rises, each revolution of the ramp has its own set of four corner notches. The notches at lower elevations are sealed once the ramp segment is no longer in active use, filled with blocks and incorporated into the pyramid mass. Only the active set of notches (at or near the current working level) remains open.

This means the pyramid, during construction, has a distinctive appearance at the corners: open platforms at intervals ascending each corner edge. These are progressively sealed from the bottom up as work advances.

## Workforce Inside the Ramp

### Team Sizes

The internal ramp demands a different labor model than the external ramp.

- **Hauling team per block**: 12-20 men in single file or paired, pulling a rope attached to the sledge. Compared to 20-40 men on an open external ramp, the team is smaller because the friction is lower (finished, maintained floor surface, consistent grade) but the corridor limits how many men can pull effectively.
- **Corner crew**: 4-6 men stationed at each active corner notch to manage the turning operation. These are semi-permanent positions.
- **Ramp maintenance crew**: 2-4 men per straight run, keeping the floor surface in condition, managing roller placement, clearing debris.
- **Water carriers**: 2-3 per straight run, wetting the surface ahead of sledges. Water must be hauled up to each level — a constant overhead.
- **Lamp tenders / ventilation managers**: 1-2 per straight run, maintaining oil lamps or mirror arrays.

**Total inside the ramp at any time**: perhaps 80-150 men (across all four straight runs and four corners of the active revolution), plus blocks in transit.

### Spacing and Throughput

The ramp can only accommodate one block in transit per straight run at a time (given the narrow width, passing is not practical). With four straight runs per revolution, up to 4 blocks can be in transit simultaneously on the active loop.

A single block transit from base to working level:
- At 60m elevation: ~800m of ramp at 7% grade. Hauling speed ~10m per minute (slow, uphill, enclosed). Transit time: ~80 minutes. Add corner turns (~5 minutes each, 4 turns if doing a full revolution): total ~100 minutes per block for a full circuit.
- At 120m elevation: ~1,500m of ramp. Transit time: ~150 minutes plus corners: ~170 minutes.

But blocks don't always travel from the base. At upper levels, a block placed on course N needs to travel only the ramp distance from the previous exit point. And above 43m, the blocks are smaller and lighter (upper courses use progressively smaller blocks, many under 1 ton).

**Estimated throughput**: 30-50 blocks per day at mid-pyramid elevations, declining to 10-20 blocks per day at upper elevations. This is slower than the external ramp phase but the blocks being placed are far fewer per course (the top quarter of the pyramid by height contains less than 2% of the total mass).

### Ventilation and Lighting

**Ventilation** is managed passively through the chimney effect: the corner notches at the top of the active loop are open to the air. Hot air rises and exits through the upper notches; cooler air is drawn in through the base entrance. During construction in the Egyptian heat (ambient temperatures of 35-40 C), the ramp interior would be hot but not dangerously so, as the thick surrounding masonry provides thermal mass that moderates temperature.

**Lighting** options:
- Oil lamps (sesame oil or animal fat) placed in wall niches at 5-10m intervals along straight runs. Produces soot — ramp walls would be blackened.
- Polished copper reflectors bouncing sunlight from the open corner notches down the straight runs. Effective for the first 20-30m from each corner; beyond that, lamp light is needed.
- The corner notch platforms themselves are fully daylit (open to the sky), providing rest and orientation points for workers.

## The Grand Gallery as Counterweight System

### The Problem of the King's Chamber Granite

The King's Chamber is located approximately 43m above ground level (notably, at the same elevation where the external ramp transitions to the internal ramp). Its ceiling consists of nine granite beams, each weighing 25-50 tons, with five relieving chambers above it capped by additional granite beams — approximately 43 granite beams totaling over 2,000 tons. These beams were quarried in Aswan (934 km upstream) and are far too heavy for the internal spiral ramp.

### Houdin's Grand Gallery Hypothesis

The Grand Gallery is a 47m-long corridor with an 8.5m-high corbelled ceiling, inclined at 26.5 degrees. Its walls contain a series of regularly-spaced notches or slots cut into the stone — 28 pairs, one every ~1.6m along both walls.

Houdin proposes the Grand Gallery functioned as a counterweight track:

1. A heavy granite beam is placed on a sledge at the base of the Grand Gallery (which opens through the ascending passage from the north face entrance).
2. A rope runs from the beam-sledge up the Gallery, over a roller or pulley at the top, and back down to a counterweight sledge loaded with dense stone blocks.
3. The counterweight descends the Gallery under gravity, pulling the granite beam upward.
4. The wall slots held wooden beams that served as cradles or braking points for the counterweight sledges, preventing runaway descent.

**The physics**: a 50-ton granite beam on a 26.5-degree incline requires a force equal to roughly 50 x sin(26.5) = ~22 tons to overcome gravity along the slope (ignoring friction). A counterweight of 25-30 tons descending the same slope generates a comparable force. Additional human effort (teams pulling ropes) makes up the difference and overcomes friction.

This system would have operated during the external ramp phase (Phase 1), when the King's Chamber level is being constructed. The granite beams are hauled up the Gallery and placed into position through internal passages before the courses above seal them in.

**The timing is critical**: all 43 granite beams for the King's Chamber and its relieving chambers must be placed before the pyramid construction rises above ~60-65m. After that, the internal passages to the chamber are sealed by the rising courses, and the Grand Gallery's role as a counterweight track is complete. It becomes a permanent (and sealed) passage within the finished pyramid.

## Advantages Over Pure External Ramp

### Material Economy

A straight external ramp reaching 146.6m at a 7% grade would need to be approximately 2.1 km long. Its volume would approach or exceed the pyramid's own volume (~2.6 million cubic meters). The ramp itself becomes a construction project as large as the pyramid.

The internal spiral ramp requires **zero additional ramp material**. The ramp corridor is a void within the pyramid mass that would otherwise be filled with stone. It is literally free in terms of material — it is space *not* filled rather than material added.

The small external ramp used for Phase 1 (reaching only 43m, not 146.6m) requires roughly 150,000-200,000 cubic meters of fill — approximately 7-8% of the pyramid's volume. This is dismantled and partly recycled as the external ramp is removed.

### Scaling

The fundamental problem with external ramps: as the pyramid grows, the ramp must grow proportionally (or worse — cubically for a straight ramp keeping constant width). Past roughly 40-50m of height, the external ramp begins to dominate the project logistics.

The internal ramp has the opposite scaling property. As the pyramid narrows at higher elevations, each revolution of the ramp is shorter. The ramp corridor stays the same cross-section (~1.6m x 2.0m) regardless of height. The number of blocks per course drops dramatically (a course at 140m elevation might contain a few hundred blocks versus tens of thousands at the base). The ramp's capacity — limited as it is — is well matched to the diminishing demand at upper courses.

This is the elegant match: the external ramp handles the massive lower courses where brute-force parallel hauling is needed, and the internal ramp handles the upper courses where throughput requirements are low but height makes external ramps impractical.

### No Disposal Problem

An external ramp of 2+ million cubic meters must be removed after the pyramid is finished. Where does the material go? The internal ramp has no disposal problem — the ramp stays inside the pyramid forever. The corner notches are sealed with a few blocks each. The base entrance is sealed. The ramp becomes an invisible internal feature.

## Scaling to the Summit

### The Upper Courses

Above 120m, the pyramid is less than 40m per side. Each course is a relatively small platform. The blocks are smaller (many under 500 kg at the very top courses). A single revolution of the internal ramp at this elevation is only ~160m long.

At these heights, even the reduced internal ramp workforce can deliver blocks efficiently. A team of 10-12 men can haul a 500 kg block up a 7% ramp inside a smooth corridor. Corner turns with sub-ton blocks are manageable by hand with levers, without the full pivoting apparatus needed for larger blocks below.

The pyramidion (capstone) is the final challenge — a single finished piece weighing perhaps 2-4 tons that must reach the summit intact. The internal ramp delivers it to the final course level, and lever/rocker techniques place it.

### What External Ramps Cannot Do

At 130m+ elevation, a straight external ramp at 7% would need to extend nearly 2 km from the pyramid. A wrapping ramp at this height would be spiraling around a pyramid face only 25m wide, with corner-turn radii too tight for loaded sledges and structural support challenges on the narrow upper faces. Neither approach is practical.

The internal ramp maintains the same geometry and operational method at 130m as at 50m. The corridor is the same width, the same grade, the same turning apparatus. Only the distance per revolution changes — and it gets shorter, not longer.

## Time Estimates at Great Pyramid Scale

### Phase 1 (External Ramp, 0-43m)

- **Mass to be placed**: approximately 4.4-4.6 million tons (~73% of total 6.1 million tons)
- **Duration**: approximately 10-12 years
- **Rate**: ~400-450 blocks/day during peak season (Akhet inundation, ~4 months), declining to 150-200 blocks/day in off-season
- **Workforce**: 20,000-25,000 during Akhet; 8,000-12,000 during Peret/Shemu
- **Concurrent with**: construction of the internal ramp corridor within each course, and hauling/placing all King's Chamber and relieving chamber granite via the Grand Gallery counterweight system

### Phase 2 (Internal Ramp, 43-146.6m)

- **Mass to be placed**: approximately 1.5-1.7 million tons (~27% of total)
- **Duration**: approximately 7-9 years
- **Rate**: 50-100 blocks/day at mid-elevations, declining to 10-30 blocks/day above 120m
- **Workforce**: 8,000-12,000 total (smaller hauling teams inside ramp, plus ongoing quarry and transport operations at base). Of these, perhaps 150-300 are working inside the ramp at any given time.
- **Concurrent with**: casing stone placement (working top-down on the exterior while structural courses are still being placed above — the casing is placed in a descending spiral as the ramp crews work upward)

### Total

Approximately 17-21 years, consistent with the traditionally cited ~20 years for the Great Pyramid and with the archaeological evidence (Merer's papyri date to late in Khufu's reign, suggesting operations were ongoing into years 26-27 of his rule).

## Operational Summary

The internal spiral ramp theory describes a two-phase construction system that exploits a fundamental insight: the hard part of pyramid building is not the bottom (where the mass is) or the top (where the height is), but the middle, where both height and mass are significant. By using an external ramp for the mass-intensive lower third and an internal ramp for the height-intensive upper two-thirds, the theory avoids the scaling failure of either approach used alone.

The system requires more skilled labor than a pure external ramp — masons must build the ramp corridor precisely into every course, corner notch platforms must be engineered for block turning, and hauling teams must operate in confined conditions with managed ventilation and lighting. But it requires far less total material, solves the ramp disposal problem entirely, and scales gracefully to the summit.

For game purposes (per DESIGN.md Theory B), this maps to a "fewer resources, more planning" playstyle: the player trades the brute-force logistics of Theory A for a system that demands skilled workers, precise coordination, and training pipeline investment. Failure modes shift from material bottlenecks to skilled labor shortages and the consequences of errors inside a confined, difficult-to-repair corridor.
